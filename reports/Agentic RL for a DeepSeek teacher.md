# Borrow FireFlash's filters, not its cluster

FireFlash teaches the owner how to build and filter agentic tasks, not how to train a giant model: of its +10.4-point gain on Terminal-Bench 4.0, **+4.4 came from the harness with no training at all**, RL added +6.0 (about four of 66 tasks), and the authors released no weights, code, tasks or graders ([FireFlash](https://agenteconomics.org/posts/agentic-rl-recipe)). Its transferable habits (gate every grader, profile tasks in the exact run configuration and keep the 15–85 % pass band, reward full and short solves, treat the harness as part of the model) cost no GPU time and apply directly to filtering DeepSeek's Elixir sessions. Improving DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 itself is out of reach at home, because **its frozen weights without the Engram tables (~293 GiB) exceed the 7-GPU machine's VRAM and RAM combined (~257 GiB)** and the P106 rigs cannot even serve it. Rented, the smallest validated run is LoRA RL on 16 H200s whose adapters reach only attention and the shared expert, at roughly $6–36k per attempt, with no published agentic-coding result on this model and single-digit gains expected on an agent its makers already trained with RL at enormous scale. It cannot pay for itself either: **DeepSeek's own API serves the base model at $0.02–0.09 per agent attempt and its terms explicitly allow distillation**, so 2,000 verified sessions cost $150–580, while a tuned teacher served on Tinker costs 3.6–7.3× more per attempt. The distillation literature then says a stronger teacher gave an 8B student nothing and a 32B student +2.9 points, while small students passed their teachers in low-resource languages only through RL against a verifier. The "agentic data dump" half of the owner's goal is reachable now, and the "improve dramatically" half belongs on the student, with `mix test` as the reward and FireFlash's recipe scaled down. One unmeasured number decides the rest, DeepSeek's pass rate on Elixir, and about $10–35 of API time on 50 mined tasks would measure it.

## FireFlash's +10.4 is 42 % harness, and none of it can be downloaded

FireFlash is full-parameter, fully asynchronous GRPO on GLM-5.3-Flash (321B total, 17.4B active, MIT) for terminal and coding agents. It ran in under five days on 64 B300s, half of them a slime trainer in BF16 and half SGLang rollout engines with FP8 weights and MTP speculative decoding ([FireFlash](https://agenteconomics.org/posts/agentic-rl-recipe)). Weights are pushed after every step, and turns sampled more than six versions earlier get no gradient. Across that staleness the trainer–sampler log-prob gap grew only from 0.065 to 0.08. Each step runs 32 tasks × 12 attempts, and each attempt gets up to 192K context, 200 turns and three hours. The reported pass@1 gains, averaged over five trials, are **Terminal-Bench 4.0 35.8 → 46.2, DeepSWE v1.1 63.4 → 68.8 and SWE-Bench Pro Verified 58.2 → 63.3**. The post lists nine authors and no affiliation, and is dated 2 November 2026 ([Agent Economics](https://agenteconomics.org/)).

The headline needs taking apart. Run in the authors' harness with unchanged weights, the base model already scored 40.2 against its official 35.8. **The harness therefore accounts for 42 % of the Terminal-Bench gain, and RL for +6.0** (my arithmetic). Terminal-Bench 4.0 has 66 tasks ([vals.ai](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/terminal-bench-4)), so RL's share is about four tasks. SWE-Bench Pro Verified has 731 instances ([SWE-Bench Pro Verified](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08149)), so its +5.1 is about 37 tasks, the statistically strongest of the three gains (my arithmetic). The DeepSWE baseline is the official mini-swe-agent entry ([GLM-5.3-Flash card](https://huggingface.co/zai-org/GLM-5.3-Flash)), so that +5.4 mixes harness and RL effects in unknown proportions. None of the RL-specific pieces has a benchmark ablation, and the results are single runs without confidence intervals. RL also raised cost per solve by about 40 %, from $1.11 to $1.56, despite the length bonus (my arithmetic from the post's own figures).

The post contradicts itself in places. Its text says 64 GPUs while its configuration table implies a cluster of about a hundred. The prose asks for five profiling rollouts per task, the table four. It has no decontamination statement. It also claims to pass "Fable 5.1" on DeepSWE, although DeepSWE's own leaderboard puts Fable 5 at 70 % ±3 %, above FireFlash's 68.8 ([DeepSWE leaderboard](https://deepswe.datacurve.ai)).

"Open recipe" here means a written description. Papers with Code lists the post with no repository, model, dataset or licence ([Papers with Code](https://paperswithcode.co/paper/131779)). About **80 % of the training mix is in-house data whose authors the post never names**. Only MiMo-TB, Tasksmith and MiMo-code, 20 % in all, are labelled public, and none of them is Elixir. A secondary write-up puts Xiaomi's MiMo RL environment release at 7,780 tasks under Apache-2.0, unconfirmed on any primary page ([Towards AI](https://pub.towardsai.net/what-xiaomis-7-780-mimo-tasks-reveal-about-training-agents-2788d4ce8a81)). The tools the post cites are open. AgentENV (MIT) runs each attempt in a Firecracker microVM and documents a single-node install that needs only Linux ≥ 6.8 and KVM ([AgentENV](https://github.com/kvcache-ai/AgentENV)). slime (Apache-2.0) supports GLM and Qwen models, but lists DeepSeek only up to V3.1 and R1 ([slime](https://github.com/THUDM/slime)).

The two cited methods hold up differently. Score centering is real, has no hyperparameters and costs little. Its MIT code is already integrated in verl, TRL, slime, SkyRL, prime-rl and Tinker ([score-centering](https://github.com/martin-marek/score-centering)), and it was shown on models from 0.6B to 30B-A3B. Its own results, though, show an FP8 sampler alone training stably at 30B. The fix matters under staleness and harsh quantization: with an FP4 KV cache, plain policy gradient collapsed, while score centering reached 52 % ([Score Centering](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20807)). The citation behind the "success offset" is loose. Terminal-RSI defines a success-prioritised reward for PPO with a learned critic and tests it only offline. FireFlash's constants, 1.5 for a solve and at most 0.25 for the best near-miss, match that paper's offline mean rewards of 1.50 and 0.24 almost exactly ([Terminal-RSI](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22192)).

On independent measurements, FireFlash also starts from a stronger terminal agent than DeepSeek. vals.ai runs every model in one harness, and on Terminal-Bench 4.0 it has GLM-5.3-Flash at 37.37 % and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 at 31.82 % ([vals.ai](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/terminal-bench-4)). DeepSeek's own figure for 1017 is 42.3, measured in Claude Code ([1017 card](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017)).

## Four FireFlash habits turn DeepSeek into an Elixir data factory

**The harness is the cheapest large lever, and DeepSeek's own paper shows it is large for V4.1 too.** On a fixed checkpoint at maximum effort, DeepSWE v1.1 scored 74.2 in mini-SWE, 69.8 in Claude Code and **66.2 in Pi**, the harness the owner's Elixir bench uses. Four versions of Claude Code spread only 1.4 points ([V4.1 report](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.19969v1)). Reasoning effort moves the score almost as much: going from 25 to 100 lifts DeepSWE from 66.0 to 74.2 at about 2.5× the output tokens, and DeepSeek says the 60–80 range keeps most of that gain at under half the token budget ([V4.1 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19969)).

The catch is that a student learns the tool protocol its sessions were recorded in, so the right move is not to switch the teacher to mini-SWE. Generate in the harness the student will run in, and copy into it what FireFlash measured: command output written to files with a truncated preview returned to the model, long `mix compile` and `mix test` runs continuing in the background instead of dying at a timeout, compaction that appends a summary instead of rewriting the prompt prefix, and prompt rules against sleep-polling and repeated rewrites. The first three cut FireFlash's per-trial cost by about a third, and the prompt rules by a further 16 % ([FireFlash](https://agenteconomics.org/posts/agentic-rl-recipe)). They matter even more on DeepSeek's price list. A cached input token costs 1/50 of an uncached one there ([DeepSeek pricing](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/quick_start/pricing)), so keeping the prefix cache through compaction separates about $0.022 from about $0.087 per off-peak attempt (my arithmetic). On the rig, where cost is time, the same changes shorten time per task, which is the project's agentic-speed metric (inference). Native tool calls also matter for this teacher. DeepSeek warns that a harness which passes tool results back as user messages can miss the context path that keeps the model's reasoning across turns ([V4 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19348)). FireFlash likewise used native tool calls with the reasoning kept.

**Every Elixir task needs FireFlash's nine gates before any model sees it.** The gates check the task, not the model. The reference fix must pass in the training sandbox through the exact training pipeline, every requirement the grader checks must be stated in the prompt, and the task must run offline and boot every time without hanging. Nothing in the environment may leak the answer, doing nothing or submitting boilerplate must score zero, and any correct solution must pass. Malformed output must get a score rather than crash the grader, and the same output must always get the same score. FireFlash's audit of tasks no rollout ever solved found that "nearly half were broken rather than hard" ([FireFlash](https://agenteconomics.org/posts/agentic-rl-recipe)). DeepSeek lists the reward hacks its own RL found, among them leaking answers from package mirror services and deleting binaries ([V4.1 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19969)). SWE-Bench Pro Verified shows how far hacking inflates scores: GLM-5.2 fell from 78.80 to 57.32 once git history, hidden tests and code-hosting domains were locked down ([SWE-Bench Pro Verified](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.08149v3)).

For tasks mined from Hex packages the risk is concrete, because the fixed release of the package under repair is already on hex.pm. The sandbox therefore has to run offline, from a dependency cache that cannot hold the target's later versions, in a fresh repository with a single commit (inference from both sources). Elixir suits the determinism gate well, since `mix test` accepts a fixed seed. Tests that depend on timing or on async ordering still need several runs before a task is admitted (inference).

**The 15–85 % band means opposite things for RL and for a data dump.** For RL it is the best-replicated ingredient in the literature. Dropping zero-variance groups gave +8 of DAPO's +20 AIME points, in the only isolated ablation ([DAPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14476v2)). Without such filtering, agentic runs report around 40 % of groups with no successes at all ([Tmax](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.23321)). For a data generator the band is a cost map rather than a filter. Tasks the teacher always solves are cheap filler, best capped at two or three sessions each as SWE-smith did ([SWE-smith](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.21798v2)). The 0–15 % tail is where extra attempts buy new coverage. Profiling the teacher on the API is cheap: five attempts on each of 1,000 tasks cost $110–225 (my arithmetic from [DeepSeek pricing](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/quick_start/pricing)).

**FireFlash's reward doubles as a rule for choosing sessions.** A solve enters at 1.5 and the best near-miss at 0.25, and the length bonus is paid only when at least two attempts solve the task ([FireFlash](https://agenteconomics.org/posts/agentic-rl-recipe)). For SFT data that means keeping full solves, preferring the shortest solve of each task, and keeping near-misses only with their wrong steps masked out of the loss. The SFT evidence agrees. In SWE-Lego, semi-resolved runs added 1.2 points, and error masking plus a curriculum ordered by turn count added another 2.8–3.8 at 8B and 32B ([SWE-Lego](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01426v2)). Adding raw failures cost 5.2 points in CLI-Universe ([CLI-Universe](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22883)). In SERA, keeping the teacher's reasoning gave 41.0 against 23.0 without it ([SERA](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20789v2)). Elixir's compiler and test runner can act as cheap step-level critics for that masking.

**DeepSeek's own V4.1 report reaches FireFlash's conclusion from the other side.** DeepSeek introduced no new post-training algorithm for V4.1. It credits nearly all its gains to three things: synthesised verifiable tasks and environments, RL across many scaffolds, and merged RL checkpoints. A pipeline of builder, setup, solver, QA and repair agents turns GitHub repositories into tasks with fail-to-pass checks ([V4.1 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19969)). That pipeline, not the RL cluster, is the part a one-person project can copy, and for Elixir it has to be built from scratch. FireFlash's public 20 % and MiMo's environments contain no Elixir. The owner's own Elixir dataset was generated with Claude ([generator](https://github.com/Cinderella-Man/elixir-sft-dataset/blob/328195bb/lib/gen_task/opus.ex)), which under the owner's data rule limits it to evaluation.

## DeepSeek V4.1 cannot be trained at home, and renting buys single digits

Only one V4.1 line exists: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and its October update, 1017. There is no V4.1-Pro ([Hugging Face collection](https://huggingface.co/collections/deepseek-ai/deepseek-v4)). The model has a 552B-parameter MoE backbone plus 196B of Engram n-gram tables. It activates 8B parameters per token in prefill and 16B in decode, has 384 routed experts and a 1M context, and calls tools in an XML-style format DeepSeek calls DSML ([V4.1 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19969)). The shipped checkpoint is about 476 GiB: 259.5 GiB of MXFP4 experts and 183.1 GiB of FP8 Engram tables ([vLLM recipe](https://recipes.vllm.ai/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash)). The weights are under plain MIT ([LICENSE](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017/blob/main/LICENSE)).

Without the Engram tables about 293 GiB of weights remain. The 7-GPU machine has about 132 GiB of VRAM and 125 GiB of RAM, so **even the frozen weights do not fit, before activations, adapters or optimiser state** (my arithmetic). No offload path closes the gap. KTransformers' LoRA, which keeps the experts on the CPU, covers only the April V4-Flash ([KTransformers v0.7.2](https://github.com/kvcache-ai/ktransformers/releases/tag/v0.7.2)); Transformers support for V4.1 is still an open pull request ([transformers #48755](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/issues/48755)); LoRA support in vLLM is an open feature request ([vLLM #57166](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/57166)); and Unsloth ships V4.1 GGUF files but no fine-tuning recipe ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/deepseek-v4.1-flash)).

Serving at home does work, one conversation at a time. dsx runs a 252 GB model file at 34.9–41.0 tok/s on two RTX 3090s, and at 31.6 tok/s with 128K of context ([dsx current state](https://github.com/lxe/dsx/blob/main/docs/current-state.md)). Its speed comes partly from an approximation that changes the computation. Its quality figure, a KL divergence of 0.196, comes from an evaluation the author tuned against. dsx uses at most six GPUs, and its CPU kernels prefer VNNI instructions, which the machine's Zen 3 CPU lacks ([dsx](https://github.com/lxe/dsx)). The other offload engine needs at least 256 GiB of RAM, twice what the machine has ([dsv41-flash-offload](https://github.com/0xSero/dsv41-flash-offload)). The P106 rigs are out entirely. Each has 54 GB of VRAM and 4 GB of host RAM, while the smallest GGUF still needs 122.4 GB for its weights outside the Engram tables. No V4.1 engine builds for Pascal's sm_61 ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/deepseek-v4.1-flash); [dsx](https://github.com/lxe/dsx)).

On rented hardware, two training paths are validated, both in the Miles framework. Miles ran full RL of V4.1-Flash on 16 GB300s and lifted a math reward from 0.51 to 0.78 in 80 steps, with the Engram tables frozen and kept in host memory ([LMSYS](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2026-09-10-deepseek-v41/)). Its LoRA RL, merged on 27 October 2026, trained on two 8-GPU H200 nodes at about 307–340 s per step with FP8 rollouts. Over 100 steps on an unstated task it raised the mean reward by about 0.11. **Its rank-16 adapters reach attention and the shared expert only, not the 384 routed experts, the indexer or Engram** ([Miles PR #4205](https://github.com/radixark/miles/pull/4205)). Other tools are less mature. ModelScope's bridge makes Engram trainable but was tested only on a four-layer slice of the model ([mcore-bridge PR #201](https://github.com/modelscope/mcore-bridge/pull/201)), NVIDIA's NeMo recipe is a 16-node image-text fine-tune ([NeMo AutoModel](https://docs.nvidia.com/nemo/automodel/model-coverage/vision-language-models/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash)), and Tinker and Fireworks list LoRA training for the 1017 checkpoint without published results ([Tinker](https://thinkingmachines.ai/tinker/); [Fireworks](https://fireworks.ai/models/deepseek-ai/deepseek-v4p1-flash-1017)).

Nobody outside DeepSeek has published an agentic-coding fine-tune or RL run of V4 or V4.1. The 65 listed finetunes of the 1017 checkpoint are abliterations and format conversions ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/models?other=base_model:finetune:deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017)). A measured run would therefore be among the first, which is an argument for doing it as research, not for saving money.

Where the adapters sit matters for Elixir. Thinking Machines found that LoRA matches full fine-tuning in policy-gradient RL down to rank 1 on math, because each RL episode carries only about one bit of information. They also found that LoRA on attention alone underperforms LoRA on the MLP layers, in RL too ([LoRA Without Regret](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/)). The only working V4.1 adapter covers attention plus one always-active MLP, the shared expert. That can reshape behaviour such as tool discipline and session length. Elixir library knowledge, if the teacher lacks it, would have to change in the routed experts or the Engram tables, which no released tool trains at scale (inference).

Numerical mismatch between trainer and sampler is the other hazard. In vLLM's experiment on DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Base, the KL divergence between trainer and inference engine rose from 0.002 to 29.9 within 100 steps in an uncorrected baseline, while a bitwise-consistent run stayed at zero ([vLLM](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-10-14-train-inference-consistency)). In GLM-5, a non-deterministic sparse-attention indexer collapsed training within a few steps, until the indexer was made deterministic and frozen ([GLM-5](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15763v2)). V4.1 has the same kind of indexer, and Miles recomputes its top-k choices during training instead of replaying the sampler's ([LMSYS](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2026-09-10-deepseek-v41/)).

Even a clean run would buy little on general benchmarks. Published RL on strong agents that were already RL-trained adds +0.3 to +10 points on the benchmark trained for. The cleanest comparison is Tmax: the same recipe gave a 9B model +9.1 on Terminal-Bench 2.1, and a 27B model starting at 43.6 only +0.3 ([Tmax](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.23321)). Composer 2.5's extra RL moved Terminal-Bench 2 only from 64 to 66 ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/cursor-composer-2-5-coding-agent-index)). DeepSeek's own scaling chart covers about 2,000 cumulative RL steps on V4.1, run with millions of concurrent sandboxes and followed by distillation from over 40 teachers ([V4.1 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19969)). An outsider's run on top of that starts in the regime where RL mostly sharpens what the model already does (inference).

Large gains have appeared only inside the training distribution. RL on 363 long-horizon tool tasks gave +17.3 on held-out tasks of the same kind, but +2.8 to +9.6 on external benchmarks ([Mehta et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00181); [summary](https://codex.danielvaughan.com/2026/08/10/cross-benchmark-generalization-agentic-rl-long-horizon-codex-cli-model-selection-transfer/)). Elixir is exactly such a narrow target, so a dramatic gain is conceivable there if V4.1 turns out weak at Elixir. Nobody has measured that yet: neither DeepSeek report has an Elixir or functional-language result ([V4.1 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19969)).

## A better teacher buys only yield, and DeepSeek's API sells yield for cents

The owner is right that a test-filtered data generator needs coverage more than reliability. A generator with hidden tests keeps every passing session, so its yield is pass@k, the chance that at least one of k attempts passes. A verifier choosing one patch at test time loses the 10–12 points between best@16 and pass@16 that DeepSWE (59 against 71) and SWE-Gym show; a data pipeline does not ([DeepSWE](https://www.together.ai/blog/deepswe); [SWE-Gym](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.21139v2)). FireFlash-style RL trains on tasks inside the 15–85 % band. A 12-attempt generator already solves each of those at least once with at least 85.8 % probability (my arithmetic). On those tasks, then, RL mostly turns two solves in twelve into eight, adding at most about 14 % coverage.

Beyond its own training tasks, the evidence on RL and coverage splits. On math, RL-trained models win at small k and lose to their own base models at large k ([Yue et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837v4)), because RL prunes correct paths along with wrong ones ([Matsutani et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.21128)). On agentic SWE work from a weak base of 11.4 %, RL raised pass@10 by 15.4 points, against 18.5 for pass@1 ([Golubev et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03501v2)). Nobody has measured this on a strong agent that has already had RL. RL also narrows a model's output: FireFlash's entropy fell from 0.46 to 0.33. Yet the number of distinct correct solutions is what drives rejection-sampling fine-tuning ([Yuan et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.01825)). A sharpened teacher would likely give more passing sessions per attempt, but less varied ones per task (inference).

DeepSeek's API serves the 1017 checkpoint as `deepseek-flash`. Per million tokens it charges $0.15 off-peak and $0.30 at peak for uncached input, $0.003 and $0.006 for cached input, and $0.60 and $1.20 for output. Peak covers only seven hours on weekdays ([DeepSeek pricing](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/quick_start/pricing)). An Elixir attempt of 20k generated, 60k uncached and 440k cached tokens costs $0.022–0.045, and an attempt twice that long $0.043–0.086 (my arithmetic). At a 30 % pass rate, **2,000 kept sessions cost $150–580, and 10,000 cost $740–2,900**.

Against those prices a tuned teacher cannot pay for itself. Raising the pass rate from 0.30 to 0.45 saves only $0.024–0.096 per kept session. The tuned teacher also cannot run on DeepSeek's API. Sampled on Tinker at $0.161 per attempt, it costs 2.4–4.9× more per kept session than the base model even at a 45 % pass rate. To break even against the API's peak price it would need a pass rate of 1.07, which is impossible (my arithmetic from [Tinker pricing](https://thinkingmachines.ai/tinker/pricing/)). Renting a node to serve even the base model costs $0.20–0.29 per attempt, 4.6–6.5× the API's peak price. That figure is my arithmetic from V4-Flash throughput measured by [Lambda](https://lambda.ai/inference-models/deepseek-ai/deepseek-v4-flash) and from list prices.

|---|---:|---:|---|

| LoRA rejection fine-tuning, 1–2 rounds (Tinker) | $0.45–4.4k | 4,700–180,000, and only if served at base prices | Tinker at $0.161 per attempt, or a dedicated deployment |

Generating at home does not change the verdict. With dsx at 32–41 tok/s, an Elixir attempt takes about 8–15 minutes of decode, prefill and tool calls. **The 7-GPU machine would then yield 1.2–2.4 kept sessions per hour, and need 35–72 days for 2,000** (my arithmetic). That is a quarter to a half of the earlier estimate of 5–10 per hour. The electricity per kept session (about €0.09–0.20 at €0.30/kWh, estimated) is close to what the API charges off-peak ($0.07–0.14), but the API runs hundreds of attempts at once.

A smaller teacher makes home generation fast. The same machine running a 30–40B-A3B teacher in vLLM made 1,177 gated sessions in 9.15 hours on all seven GPUs ([agentic data pipeline](https://github.com/Cinderella-Man/llms-on-crypto-mining-rigs/blob/v3/docs/research/notes/agentic_data_pipeline.md)). At a 30 % pass rate, 2,000 kept sessions would take about 52 hours and €25, but they would come from a weaker teacher whose habits the student would learn. A P106 rig runs Flint at a measured 44.6–45.9 tok/s ([Flint](https://github.com/Cinderella-Man/llms-on-crypto-mining-rigs/blob/v3/models/flint/README.md)), about 2–3 kept sessions per hour (estimate). The rigs make better evaluators than teachers.

A better teacher earns its cost in only one case: Elixir tasks the base never solves. Even there, sampling beats training. Take a task with a 2 % pass rate per attempt, sampled until it first passes, with a cap of 50 attempts. It gets covered 64 % of the time, at $1.1–4.3 per newly covered task on the API (my arithmetic). Tasks at exactly zero cannot be reached by more sampling, nor by fine-tuning the teacher on its own successes, since it has none. FireFlash's audit says to check first whether those tasks are broken.

For the tasks that survive the audit, two probes are cheap. The first is privileged hints: P2T showed the teacher the developer's reference patch during data curation only, and raised its student's SWE-bench Verified score by up to 10.8 points ([P2T](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.21996)). Every commit-mined Elixir task has such a patch, provided the hint is stripped from the stored session. The second is a bigger, older model. DeepSeek V4-Pro-0813 costs about four times as much per attempt and scores 14.14 % against V4.1-Flash's 31.82 % on vals.ai's Terminal-Bench 4.0 ([vals.ai](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/terminal-bench-4)), but V4.1-Flash-Base trails V4-Pro-Base on the knowledge test SimpleQA-Verified, 42.3 against 55.2 ([V4.1 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19969)). An Elixir tail limited by library knowledge is the one place that difference could show (inference).

Documentation in the prompt has to be measured rather than assumed. AGENTS.md-style context files tended to lower success on SWE-bench tasks while raising cost by over 20 % ([context files study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988v1)).

### The data rule admits DeepSeek's weights and its own API, not every host

The weights are under plain MIT, with no acceptable-use policy and no restriction on outputs ([LICENSE](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017/blob/main/LICENSE)). DeepSeek's Open Platform terms, in force since 29 April 2026, assign the rights in outputs to the user. They expressly allow using outputs to train other models, distillation included, and I found no non-compete clause ([DeepSeek terms](https://cdn.deepseek.com/policies/en-US/deepseek-open-platform-terms-of-service.html)). A third-party tracker reports an anti-distillation clause, but it belongs to an older V3 model licence and conflicts with both texts above ([ConductAtlas](https://conductatlas.com/platform/deepseek/)). Fireworks is the counter-example. Its terms give the user ownership of outputs (§3.2) but bar using any content available through the service for machine-learning training (§2.2(c)) ([Fireworks terms](https://fireworks.ai/terms-of-service)). Sessions generated on Fireworks should stay out unless Fireworks confirms otherwise in writing. I have not read Tinker's terms on output use, nor the general terms of the GPU rental hosts. None of this is legal advice.

The owner's rule is stricter than any of these terms, and three gates follow from it. DeepSeek is not yet an approved writer, so its sessions, reasoning included, need the owner's explicit approval before they become training data. Task statements must still come from approved writers or from the language's own tools. And the repositories behind the owner's Elixir bench stay out of task mining ([bench results](https://cinderella-man.github.io/elixir-swe-bench/results.json)). Each dataset should record the model version, the licence and the date of the terms. It should also carry two provenance caveats. DeepSeek does not disclose its post-training data ([NVIDIA model card](https://docs.api.nvidia.com/nim/reference/nvidia-deepseek-v4_1-flash)). OpenAI has alleged, with no finding so far, that DeepSeek trained on OpenAI outputs ([technology.org](https://www.technology.org/2026/02/13/deepseek-may-have-trained-on-openais-outputs-and-openai-is-not-amused/)).

## Students gain little from a stronger teacher and a lot from a verifier

How much a dramatically better teacher is worth depends on the student, and for a 9B it is close to nothing. SERA is the one controlled agentic-coding comparison. There, the stronger GLM-4.6 teacher gave a Qwen3-32B student +2.9 points on SWE-bench Verified (49.5 against 46.6) and an 8B student nothing (31.7 against 31.7) ([SERA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20789)). Outside agentic work the pattern repeats. QwQ-32B taught a Qwen 7B student better than DeepSeek-R1 did, despite R1's higher scores ([OpenThoughts](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04178)); a teacher 10 AIME points stronger left a 4B student at the same level ([Hugging Face TRL](https://huggingfacetb-trl-distillation-trainer.hf.space/)); and students below about 3–7B lose from strong teachers and long traces ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12143v3)). A better teacher therefore pays through yield, through coverage of tasks a weaker teacher cannot solve, and through 30B-class students, not through imitation quality for a 9B.

DeepSeek and the likely students, Ornith-1.5 and other Qwen3.5/3.6-family models, differ in tokenizer, pre-training and tool format, which rules out the strongest distillation method for now. In on-policy distillation the student samples and the teacher scores every token. It beat RL in Qwen3: an 8B reached 74.4 on AIME'24 against RL's 67.6, using 1,800 GPU-hours against 17,920 ([Qwen3](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09388)). But it needs the teacher's log-probs on the student's tokens, and the versions that work across tokenizers recover less. GOLD recovered 60 % of the teacher's gain from Qwen to Llama, against more than 80 % within one tokenizer, on a single-turn task ([GOLD](https://huggingfaceh4-on-policy-distillation.hf.space/)). A teacher from a different model family weakens it even after tokenizer alignment ([CompassOPD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10154)), and in multi-turn tool use the student's errors compound across turns ([SOD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07725)). No paper tests all three penalties together, so DeepSeek-to-Qwen on-policy distillation is a research project, not a recipe.

Plain trajectory SFT does work across model families. 6,000 DeepSeek-V4-Pro trajectories took Qwen3-32B to 31.2 on Terminal-Bench 2.0, against 33.4 from Kimi-K2.6 trajectories ([CLI-Universe](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22883)). In practice each DeepSeek session is re-rendered in the student's chat template, with the DSML tool calls converted and the reasoning moved into the student's thinking format. A same-family second stage, such as Ornith-1.5-35B teaching a 9B that shares its tokenizer, is a hypothesis with no agentic evidence yet.

Every student that beat its teacher in a language the teacher was weak at did so through RL against a verifier. In Fortran, a Claude Sonnet 4 teacher solved 12 problems; a 4B trained on its 1,987 solutions solved 2–3, and a 4B trained with RL solved 15 ([Agnostics](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.04865)). In Verilog, a 7B distilled from DeepSeek-R1 reached 56.2 % and rose to 72.9 % after RL, reportedly 8.1 points above R1 ([CodeV-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24183)). Two other findings point the same way. Distilling from a strong RL-trained model beats running RL on a small one by a wide margin: R1-Distill-Qwen-32B scored 72.6 on AIME, against 47.0 for RL from the same base ([DeepSeek-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948v1)). And Apple's distillation scaling laws find that training a teacher for a single student rarely pays ([Busbridge et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08606)). Together they reorder the owner's plan. Distil from the teacher as it is, then push the student past it with `mix compile` and `mix test` as the reward.

The smallest measured agentic RL runs, which show what FireFlash looks like scaled down, use 16 accelerators. Tmax took a 9B from 20.5 to 29.6 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 with two H100 training nodes and six inference nodes over one to two days, plus about $3,150 of sandbox fees ([Tmax](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.23321)). Miles took an SFT-warmed Qwen3-30B-A3B from 12.8 to 27.5 on a 100-task SWE-bench Verified subset, with 64 trajectories per step for 145 steps on two 8-GPU nodes ([LMSYS/AMD](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2026-10-22-miles-mi355x-agentic-rl)). Nobody has published multi-turn coding RL on consumer GPUs. An earlier estimate, built on a single third-party RTX 3090 measurement and not checked here, puts 64 trajectories × 200 steps for a 9B at about nine days on five 3090s ([Zagitta](https://github.com/Zagitta/gpu-unlock/issues/75)).

Hosted LoRA RL on a 35B-A3B student is cheap by comparison. At Tinker's Qwen3.6-35B-A3B prices, 100 steps of 128 trajectories cost about $2.1–2.4k, roughly a third of the same run on V4.1 (my arithmetic from [Tinker pricing](https://thinkingmachines.ai/tinker/pricing/)). Ornith is not on Tinker's price list, though, and I have not read Tinker's terms. W&B's serverless RL is free for training during its preview but lists only Qwen3 14B and 30B-A3B ([W&B](https://wandb.ai/site/reinforcement-learning)).

The FireFlash pieces worth carrying over to student RL are the pass band, taken from profiling the student itself; groups of 8–12 attempts per task with uniform-reward groups dropped; the success offset and the length bonus; the 2,048-token floor on loss normalisation; and rising entropy as the early warning of instability. MoE students also need routing replay and an FP32 output head ([ScaleRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13786); [R3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11370)). Two cautions apply. In one SWE run, dropping trajectories that overflowed the context removed the negative examples of looping, and loops became more frequent ([Golubev et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03501v2)). And leaving out the KL penalty is a convention that no agentic paper has ablated; Cursor keeps a k1 KL term ([Composer 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24477v2)).

Data volume decides how much DeepSeek time the student actually needs. SERA's Qwen3-32B gained about 2.5 points per doubling from 400 to 16,000 trajectories, with no knee. About 8,000 trajectories from one repository let it match its teacher on Django and SymPy, though not on Sphinx ([SERA](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20789v2)). General agentic skill can come from clean open sets instead. Nebius released 32,161 successful runs of Qwen3-Coder-480B (Apache-2.0) on real GitHub issues under CC-BY-4.0, and they took Qwen3-30B-A3B from 25.2 to 49.7 % on SWE-bench Verified ([Nebius](https://nebius.com/blog/posts/openhands-trajectories-with-qwen3-coder-480b)). That leaves DeepSeek's budget for Elixir sessions in the low thousands, which the API covers for hundreds of dollars.

## Six stages, cheapest first, each with a stop line

The order below runs first the measurement that can settle the teacher question, and leaves teacher training for whatever cheaper levers cannot reach. The owner's gates apply throughout: approving DeepSeek as a writer, any download (dsx's 252 GB file included), API spending, and a registry row for every idea. Costs are estimates unless marked as measured. The thresholds are judgement calls, to be fixed before each run. DeepSeek's rate limits for bulk agent traffic have not been checked.

| Stage | Where | Cost (est.) | Stop line or decision |
|---|---|---:|---|
| 1. **Measure the teacher on Elixir.** V4.1-Flash-1017: pass@1 and pass@8 on 50 mined tasks; three seeds on the owner's 20-case bench in Pi; Pi against a minimal harness; effort 70 against 100 | DeepSeek API, off-peak | ~$10–35, plus bench runs | If V4.1 does not beat the best local teacher (Flash-Next solved 10–11 of 20 on the bench) on kept sessions per dollar and on coverage at k = 8, the local teacher writes the data and DeepSeek drops out |
| 2. **Harden the Elixir task factory.** The nine gates as CI, an offline dependency cache, profiling with at least 5 attempts per task, an audit of never-solved tasks | 7-GPU machine, CPU and sandbox | CPU days | If the audit finds most never-solved tasks broken, fix the factory before sampling more |
| 3. **Fill the data dump.** Student harness with FireFlash's habits; 8 attempts per task, up to 50 on unsolved tasks; stripped hints on the tail; shortest solves first; 2–3 sessions per task; reasoning kept | DeepSeek API, off-peak | $150–580 per 2,000 kept sessions | Stop sampling tasks that reached their cap; stop the tail when the cost per newly covered task passes ~$5 |
| 4. **Student SFT.** A 9B recipe pilot, then Ornith-1.5-35B, on DeepSeek Elixir sessions plus clean general sets | 7-GPU machine | ~23 h per 2,000-session epoch for a 9B (est., ±40 %) | At 500 sessions, stop if the tuned model falls below its base or malformed tool calls rise |
| 5. **Student RL** with FireFlash's recipe scaled down, with the pass band profiled on the rigs | 7-GPU machine, Tinker, or rented 16-GPU nodes | ~9 days per 64 × 200 at home (est.); ~$2.1–2.4k per 100 steps of 128 hosted | Close RL unless the held-out repository lane gains at least 3 points, or time per solved task clearly drops |
| 6. **Teacher training, gated.** One LoRA rejection fine-tuning round first; LoRA RL only as a research run | Tinker; Miles on 16 H200s | $0.45–2.2k; $6–22k | Only if about 100 or more audited tasks stay unsolved at k ≈ 50 after hints, and at least two students will use the data; judge on coverage at k = 16 on held-out tasks, never on pass@1 |

The rigs' natural job in this plan is evaluation. FireFlash profiles every task with several rollouts in the exact run configuration, and for student RL that is pure inference. A rig serving the quantized student can estimate each task's pass rate and keep the 7-GPU machine free for training. A quantized student's pass rates only approximate those of the BF16 policy being trained (inference).

A more speculative use follows from FireFlash's first lesson, that idle GPUs cost more than stale rollouts. The rigs could serve as asynchronous rollout workers for student RL, with truncated importance sampling and score centering correcting their stale, quantized samples. Score centering's own tests support the idea: with an INT8 sampler and an INT4 KV cache, it still reached 30 % training accuracy, against 12 % for truncated importance sampling ([Score Centering](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20807)). Three things stand against it. llama.cpp on Pascal is a far larger mismatch than anything that paper tested. Swapping LoRA adapters on the rig's kernels has never been verified. And one stream per rig is slow. It belongs in the queue as a parked idea with a cheap first test: measure the log-prob gap between a rig-served student and its BF16 trainer.

## Conclusion

FireFlash and DeepSeek's V4.1 report come from opposite ends: an outside group post-training someone else's model on 64 GPUs, and a model's makers running millions of sandboxes. Both credit their gains to the environment pipeline, not to the optimiser. For this project, that makes an Elixir task factory with hardened graders the asset that compounds. The same tasks profile the teacher, select its sessions, supply the student's RL pool and form the held-out lane, and they outlive any one model generation. DeepSeek-V4.1 becomes a cheap, licence-clean oracle paid per attempt, rather than a model to own and retrain.

The owner's goal survives with its halves swapped. "Improve the teacher, then distil" becomes "distil from the teacher as it is, then push the student past it with RL against a verifier". The dramatic gains in the evidence come from the student's verifier stage, not from the teacher. One finding could reverse this: an Elixir tail that DeepSeek cannot reach even with 50 attempts and hints. Such a tail is exactly the narrow, in-distribution target where RL on a strong model has shown large gains. A LoRA RL run on V4.1 would then be justified, and it would also be a public first.
